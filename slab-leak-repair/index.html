Why a pipe fails where nobody can see it

Water lines under a slab sit in contact with gravel, sand and the edges of cured concrete. Every time hot water runs, the pipe grows slightly, and every time it stops, it shrinks. Over years that movement rubs a thin spot, and the pressure inside eventually finds it.

Chemistry adds to the wear. Local water is hard, averaging 12 grains per gallon in the 2025 district report, and scale and corrosion shorten the life of fittings and thin-walled copper. That is why one failure often hints at more to come, which matters when you weigh a spot repair against bigger work.

Warm spots, hot floors and a running meter

  • A section of tile or hardwood feels warm even in a cool house.
  • The water heater seems to run all day and the hot water is weaker.
  • I can hear water running under the floor when everything is off.
  • The meter turns even with the house empty.
  • The bill rose and I cannot point to a reason.
  • Grout is darkening or a baseboard is swelling along one wall.

A hot water line is the usual source of a warm spot, since heat travels up through the concrete. A leak on a cold line shows up as dampness, not warmth, so a floor that stays cool does not clear the slab. The pattern across rooms tells the plumber which line to test first, and the full search is described under leak detection.

When water shows up under floorboards or carpet

Wet carpet near an exterior wall, lifting laminate, or boards that have started to cup are late symptoms. By the time you see them, water has traveled through the subfloor, and mold can start in damp padding.

Pull back a corner of wet carpet if you can, set a fan on the area, and photograph everything. That evidence helps both the repair plan and any claim. Do not cut into the slab yourself, because a hammer drill can strike a line that is still under pressure.

Spot repair, reroute or repipe: choosing the fix

Three ways to fix a slab leak.
OptionWhat it involvesBest when
Spot repairOpen the floor over the leak, cut out the bad section and splice in new pipeThe leak is isolated and the rest of the line looks sound.
RerouteAbandon the failed line and run a new one through walls or the atticThe line is long, or the floor above is expensive to open.
RepipeReplace the whole supply system with new pipeSeveral leaks, or old pipe that keeps failing in new places.

A reroute avoids breaking the floor, but it puts pipe in the walls and ceiling, so the patching moves instead of disappearing. A repipe costs the most up front and ends the cycle of repeat visits. If you are leaning that way, repiping covers what a whole-house replacement involves.
