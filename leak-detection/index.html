How a leak gets found without tearing out walls

  1. STEP 01

    Sort inside from outside.

    With every fixture off, the plumber watches the meter and then closes the main to see which side of the house the loss is on.

  2. STEP 02

    Pressure test each branch.

    Sections of supply line are capped and watched on a gauge. A branch that cannot hold pressure is where the leak lives.

  3. STEP 03

    Listen and scan.

    A ground microphone picks up the hiss of a pressurized pipe, and a thermal camera shows warm lines or cold, wet drywall behind paint.

  4. STEP 04

    Mark it and explain it.

    You get the spot marked, the likely cause, and the repair options, with a clear price before work starts.

The meter keeps turning with every tap off

You can run the first test yourself. Shut off every faucet and appliance, including the ice maker, then watch the small dial or triangle on the water meter. If it keeps moving, water is leaving the pipes somewhere. Drop a little food coloring in each toilet tank first, because a silent flapper leak looks exactly like a pipe leak on the meter.

A bill that jumps with no change in habits is the other clue. Ask SMWD Customer Care whether it can show your usage by period, since a slow climb points to a growing seep and a sudden step points to a break.

Ceiling stains, damp walls and wet attic insulation

A brown ring on a ceiling lies about location. Water runs along joists and pipes and drops several feet from where it escaped, so the stain marks the end of the trail, not the start. If it grows when a shower runs, suspect a supply or drain line. If it grows only after rain, it is a roof problem, and with about 13.6 inches of rain a year here, noting the weather saves a wasted visit.

Inside walls, a moisture meter and thermal camera draw the wet zone before anyone cuts, and the opening is often no bigger than a hand. In attics, the culprit is often not a pipe at all but an air conditioner's condensate line or a water heater relief line. In historic wood buildings like those along Los Rios Street, years of added and altered plumbing mean tracing the line first, then cutting.

If a ceiling is bulging with water, put a bucket below and pierce the lowest point with a screwdriver so it drains in a controlled way. Do this only with the room's power off.

Under the house and in the crawlspace

Crawlspace leaks announce themselves by smell and sound before sight. A musty odor, a soft or bouncy patch of floor, or running water heard in the quiet of night all point downward. The plumber goes under with a moisture meter and a light, and follows each supply and drain line, including the water heater's drain.

Homes on a concrete slab have no crawlspace, so those leaks are traced by sound and heat instead.

Buried pipe and the line from the street

The service line runs from the meter to the house, usually under lawn or hardscape. When it leaks, you may see a green, boggy strip, hear water running that matches no fixture, or find the meter spinning with the main closed. That last sign is decisive: if the dial turns after you close the main at the house, the leak is in the buried stretch.

Finding it takes acoustic correlation, where sensors on two points triangulate the noise, plus a tracer if needed. Call 811 before any digging. In general the pipe on the house side of the meter is the owner's responsibility, but confirm with the district that serves your address, and never touch fittings on the street side of the meter.

Sprinklers, pools and the meter box

Irrigation leaks hide in valve boxes and under mulch. A zone that keeps weeping after the timer ends, or a station that moves the meter more than its heads should, points to a broken lateral underground. We find where the line fails and tell you plainly if the fix belongs to your landscaper.

For a pool or spa, try the bucket test: float a bucket on the step with its water level marked to match the pool, and compare after a day. Evaporation drops both equally in this mild climate, while a leak drops the pool more. We pressure test the supply and return plumbing, though cracks in the shell belong to a pool specialist.

Water standing in the meter box lid usually means a fitting at the meter or shutoff is seeping.

Low pressure with no clear cause

If pressure is weak at one fixture only, scale is the likely cause. SMWD's 2025 report puts the average hardness at 12 grains per gallon, which is hard water, so aerators and showerheads clog.
